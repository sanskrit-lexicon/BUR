## Page 02 — Préface, 1 (vol. 1)

<sub>Source (scan): [bur_Page_013_Image_0001.png](https://sanskrit-lexicon.uni-koeln.de/scans/csldev/csldoc/build/dictionaries/prefaces/burpref/burpref02.html)</sub>

This Dictionary was conceived with the aim of facilitating Oriental studies and the researches that relate to them. To attain this end, several means have been employed which the public will appreciate, and of which the principal are as follows.

The state of Sanskrit studies and the needs of philology required that the work be complete enough to take the place of the great Dictionaries of Wilson and of Saint Petersburg for those who cannot procure them. It will be seen that it does in fact contain far more material than the first edition of Wilson and, a fortiori, than the lexicons or vocabularies that are abridgments of it. One will find in it many Vedic or Buddhist terms, as well as a large number of plant or animal names and everyday expressions that are entirely wanting in the latter. Nevertheless, this Dictionary, having to remain within the ordinary conditions of classical books, does not claim to be complete: one will not find in it those fanciful compounds that abound in Indian authors and whose sense is known once one possesses their elements: the simple words being given each in its place, it will always be easy to translate one of these compounds; but we give those which have a particular and, so to speak, local meaning, which analysis alone would not discover.

Alphabetical order is followed in this work. There are in fact two kinds of Dictionaries: those that one might call empirical, which presuppose no prior knowledge of the language, and scientific dictionaries, addressed to persons who already know it. The latter present the words classified by families, and, if they were well made, they would put under each root all the terms that derive from it, without exception; the language would thus be divided, according to the method of the naturalists, into a series of natural groups or synoptic tables. The others simply follow alphabetical order; only, to obviate, at least in part, the inconveniences of this system, they offer the root beside the word whenever that is possible or necessary.

In each article one may present the meanings according to their order of chronological succession, and thus make a historical dictionary of the language. In the present state of Oriental studies, we believe that a work of this kind is almost impossible for Sanskrit. But as most Sanskrit words have their root in the language itself, one can almost always, starting from the etymology, classify the various senses of a word in their order of logical derivation. This is what we have tried to do whenever it has proved possible.

Moreover, as many Sanskrit adjectives can be taken substantively either in the m., or in the f., or in the n., these various uses and meanings of one and the same word have been ranged in a common article. Such is not the rigorous usage of classical books: but it is known that Sanskrit grammars treat all declinable words simultaneously under the general title of *nouns*; we have followed this procedure, which is moreover very rational.

Compound verbs stand at their alphabetical place, as in all ordinary dictionaries. Only, as their conjugation almost never differs in Sanskrit from that of the simple verbs, it would have been to swell the volume uselessly to repeat the tenses of the verbs everywhere; to know them, one will have to refer to the simple verb, which always accompanies its own root. For example, to have the tenses of *upakaromi*, one will have to look up the root कृ *kṛ*, where the simple verb *karomi* is conjugated. But whenever the prefix causes a modification of the radical, the special article gives the forms, in appearance irregular, that result from it.

In every philological study it is necessary to be able to recognize the elements of words; they never rise to more than five: the prefix, the root, the suffix, the grammatical flexion and the euphonic or linking letters. Thus in *prākṛtena* one finds the two prefixes *pra* and *ā* contracted, the root *kṛ*, the suffix *ta* and the flexion *eṇa* where the *n* is modified by the influence of *ṛ*. Now the prefixes and the roots are given in their alphabetical order; grammar treats of the flexions and of euphony. There remain the suffixes: a complete list of them will be found at the end of this volume.

It is followed by a list of the roots classified according to their final letter and their principal analogies. The groups they form make it possible to follow them in their various transformations. This work can be pushed further than we have wished to do; it would greatly reduce the number of primordial roots to which one would arrive by studying them in the other Aryan languages.

Simple suffixes may combine with one another and give rise to compound suffixes indicating more or less numerous derivations within the same word.

The simplest suffixes consist of a short vowel, which the grammatical flexions modify in various ways.

The other simple suffixes, formed of a consonant and a vowel, are distinguished from one another by the place the consonant occupies relative to the vowel. There are thus in all three classes of suffixes, the number of which, for the last two, could still be reduced by analysis.
